#### 2.2.4 A Maximum of Storage Capacity for $\mathbf{C O}_{2}$ _with $\mathbf{S O}_{2}$ 

For impurities which have higher critical temperature than that of $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ _,no storage capacity minimum would occur in the corresponding $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ mixture. Rather, a maximum can appear based on an analysis similar to that for the non-condensable impurities. This has been verified with calculated results for the $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ mixture with 2.9 vol %$\mathrm{S O_{2}}$ _(the $\mathrm{C a n s o l v}^{\circledast}$ absorption system case presented in the previous IEA GHG report PH4/32).$\mathrm{A s}$ ,shown in Figure 2.6, a maximum is seen at about 11$\mathtt{M P a}$ , where the storage capacity is increased by over 5%.It should be noted again that the storage capacity is based on pure $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ (see discussion for Equation 2.5). It is, therefore, interesting to see that in this peak region, the $\mathrm{CO_{2}-based}$ capacity is above that of pure $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ . In other words,$\mathrm{S O}_{2}$ does not take any but creates space for $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ . This can be rationalized from the consideration that $\mathrm{S O}_{2}$ decreases average distance between the molecules of the mixture – an opposite effect of that of the non-condensable gases. This can be taken as one positive effect of the presence of $\mathrm{S O}_{2}$ on $\mathrm{C O}_{2}$ storage. However,$\mathrm{S O}_{2}$ causes more concern with regard to its chemical effects on formation rocks. This will be discussed later.
